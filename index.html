      Science notes

      What is accurate here

      • Enzymes are drawn as chains of amino acids folded into a 3-D shape held by weak interactions between R-groups: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and a hydrophobic cluster — plus one covalent disulfide bond between two cysteines.
      • Heat makes every particle move and vibrate more (kinetic energy → more collisions → faster reaction) until vibration starts breaking the weak intramolecular bonds. The shape — including the active site — is lost: denaturation. The peptide bonds and the sequence are untouched.
      • pH changes the charges on acidic (–COO⁻) and basic (–NH₃⁺) R-groups, breaking or re-making ionic bonds. Mild changes can be reversible; extremes denature the enzyme permanently. The covalent S–S bond survives both.
      • Hydrolysis consumes water (watch the blue droplet fly IN as amylase or maltase cuts). Condensation releases water (droplet OUT at the synthase, and in Build mode).
      • Denaturation is usually permanent: cooling the beaker does not refold a cooked enzyme.
      • A competitive inhibitor has a shape close enough to the substrate's to sit in the active site and block it, without being changed itself.
      • Optimum conditions are protein-specific. The enzymes here happen to like ~37 °C and pH 7 — but remember pepsin, whose optimum is stomach acid at pH ≈ 1.5.

      Deliberate simplifications

      • Enzyme and molecule shapes are cartoons, not real structures. Enzymes here are ~16 amino acids so you can see every bead and bond — real amylase is a single chain of ~500. The fold shown (weak bonds pinning a shape with an active site, hydrophobic core inside) is the real principle; the outline is not the real molecule.
      • Starch is drawn as a short straight chain of glucose hexagons. Real starch is hundreds to thousands of units — helical amylose plus branched amylopectin (B1.1.5).
      • Real amylase cuts bonds inside the chain, releasing maltose and short fragments; here each binding event frees exactly one maltose. One binding → one cut → release — the enzyme must bind again to cut again.
      • Real starch synthase does not run condensation for free: cells first activate glucose using ATP (as ADP-glucose). Condensation always has to be paid for (B1.1 → C1).
      • "Maltase" is the everyday name for the enzyme that hydrolyses maltose; in your gut it sits in the membrane of intestinal cells.
      • Numbers of molecules stand in for concentration; the graph counts them.
